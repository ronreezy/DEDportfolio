Stereotypes in Comedy

This week I watched two crazy movies that I don’t think could be made today: 1986’s Soul Man and 2004’s White Chicks. These movies base their entire premise on racial and gender impersonation, and both have become controversial in hindsight. Watching them back-to-back highlights not only how comedy changes over time, but how the social climate influences what is considered acceptable or funny.

Soul Man tells the story of a white student who takes tanning pills to appear Black in order to receive a scholarship meant for African Americans. How did this get made? Who even had the audacity to make this script? It came out during the Reagan administration, a time debates about affirmative action and racial equity were particularly heated. The main character, Mark, benefits from racism without every really experiencing it. The film uses Black stereotypes, like clothing, slang, and overreactions, for comedic effect, but it doesn’t really talk to the problems that real black people face on a day to day. Adding in a romantic interest as a way to prove the point never quite landed for me as I was just so offput by the dude in Black face. I just couldn’t get past that.

White Chicks, released in the early 2000s, flips the formula with two Black FBI agents going undercover as wealthy white women. It exaggerates the stereotypical “white girl” persona of the Paris Hilton and Nicole Richie era. I was high schooler during that time and remember seeing these Barbie women on TV and at the mall. In contrast to Soul Man, White Chicks focuses less on race and more on mocking privilege, image-obsession and extreme wealth. While it still leans on the gender and racial caricature, the punchlines are more about how absurd and artificial the characters’ worlds are. And for some reason, I can easily accept Marlon and Damon Wayans going white, then I can a white dude doing the opposite.

Both films use stereotypes, but they use them very differently. Soul Man tries and fails IMO) to make a point about racism by putting a white man in blackface, which undermines its message entirely. Robert Downey Jr. makes it work in Tropic Thunder because he brings it up as being problematic, in the movie. There is meta-comedy with Kirk Lazurus’s performance that is missing in Soul Man. White Chicks on the other hand isn’t trying to make a social statement so much as it’s using satire to poke fun at the ritzy superficial culture of the ultra-wealthy. Neither film would be made the same way today, and for good reason, but watching them now offers a chance to reflect on how stereotypes in comedy reflect the values and blind spots of their time.
