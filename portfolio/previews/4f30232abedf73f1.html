RIGHT COLUMN

THE 12 TERMS

Quality information If you would not cite it in a court argument, ask whether it belongs in your assignment.

Credible resources A source earns credibility when you can answer three things: who wrote it, why they know, and when.

Diverse perspectives The blind spot you cannot see is the one someone standing elsewhere can.

Logical reasoning The path from evidence to conclusion should have no gap a student cannot explain.

Innovative approaches When the manual runs out, this is what takes over.

Cultural impacts A decision that works for everyone in the room may not work for everyone affected by it.

Relevance At least 80 percent of what you cite should directly support the main argument. If it does not connect, it does not count.

Feasibility A great idea that cannot be resourced is not a plan. Name three things you need and three things that could go wrong.

Efficiency Getting the same result for less. If a redesign saves 15 percent in time or cost, show the math. That is the standard.

Validity At least 90 percent of your sources should be peer-reviewed. If you cannot verify the method, you cannot trust the conclusion.

Inclusivity Who does your design leave out? Name them. Then fix it.

Disparate cultural impact The same rule applied equally can still land unequally. Track the difference across at least three groups.

SIMON SAYS

That is Alice.

They enrolled at Clover Park in 2033. First person in their family to walk through those doors. They worked a night shift to make it happen and showed up every morning anyway. When they got here, they did everything right.

Their college success course did its job. Alice learned how to find quality information, not just whatever Google surfaces first, but sources worth trusting. They learned to tell the difference between a credible resource and noise. They learned to seek out diverse perspectives instead of stopping at the first answer that confirmed what they already thought. That is the foundation. That is the first floor.

Watch them in that classroom. Hand up. Confident. Engaged. Every mark hit. Every assignment turned in. By every measure the program used, Alice was exactly the student you would want.

But here is what I know that Alice does not know yet.

Being a strong student and being a critical thinker are not the same thing. One is about performing well inside the system. The other is about knowing what to do when the system runs out of answers. Alice was brilliant at the first. Nobody had asked them to practice the second.

Not yet.

WHY THIS MATTERS

In 1995, researcher David Boud made an argument that still holds up thirty years later: students learn what they are assessed on. Not what they are taught. Not what appears on the syllabus. What gets measured.

When assessments ask students to recall information, students develop the habit of recall. When assessments ask students to analyze, evaluate, and create, to do something with what they know rather than simply repeat it, students develop the capacity to think under pressure.

This is not a criticism of recall. Remembering matters. Understanding matters. They are the ground floor. But Boud's point is that the floor is not the building. If the assessment never asks a student to climb, the student has no reason to climb. They will be genuinely excellent at exactly what was asked of them, and genuinely unprepared for everything else.

Alice was unprepared for everything else.

The question Boud asks every instructor is simple: look at your last assignment. What habit does it build? If a student completed it perfectly, what can they now do that they could not do before? If the honest answer is "remember the right answer," that is not a failure of the student. That is a signal about the assessment.

Source: Boud, D. (1995). Enhancing Learning Through Self-Assessment. Routledge.

SIMON SAYS

That is Professor Nomis.

He taught Autonomous Systems for fifteen years. He knew every system, every diagnostic sequence, every failure mode the field had documented. He was good at what he did. And somewhere along the way, he had decided the CWLO vocabulary was someone else's framework for someone else's classroom.

He was not dismissive about it. He did not throw the alignment handout away. He just set it aside. He did not see the connection between "disparate cultural impact" and a Tier-2 diagnostic sequence. That language seemed borrowed from another discipline. It felt like overhead.

Here is what he did not know. The twelve terms in the Critical Thinking CWLO are not administrative decoration. They are the names of skills the workforce actually needs from graduates. Logical reasoning is what a technician uses when two diagnostic outputs contradict each other. Feasibility is what an engineer applies when a theoretically correct solution cannot be resourced. Disparate cultural impact is what a coordinator weighs when a decision affects the overnight crew differently than the day shift.

Nomis was already teaching all of those skills in practice. He just never named them. And because he never named them, he never assessed them. Because he never assessed them, Alice never practiced them as skills. They practiced them as content. That is not the same thing.

A student who can name a concept is not the same as a student who can use it under pressure. The assessment is where that difference gets built, or does not.

WHY THIS MATTERS

College-wide learning outcomes exist because workforce research consistently identifies a gap between what credentials signal and what graduates can actually do. The gap is not about knowledge. Graduates know things. The gap is about transfer: the ability to apply what you know to situations the curriculum never explicitly covered.

The Critical Thinking CWLO at Clover Park identifies twelve skills organized across three levels of Bloom's Taxonomy. The first level, Remember and Understand, covers foundational habits: identifying quality information, distinguishing credible resources, and recognizing diverse perspectives. The second level, Apply and Analyze, requires students to use logical reasoning, propose innovative approaches, and attend to cultural impacts in real-world contexts. The third level, Evaluate and Create, asks students to assess relevance, feasibility, efficiency, validity, inclusivity, and disparate cultural impact across complex, multi-stakeholder scenarios.

These levels are not abstract. They map directly to the judgment calls workers face in technical, health, business, and public-service roles. The CWLO is not asking instructors to abandon their discipline. It is asking them to name what their discipline already requires, and to build that naming into the assessment so students practice it explicitly.

When an instructor opts out, even for principled reasons, the cost is not visible at the time. Students still pass. Grades still reflect competence. The gap appears when the graduate encounters a situation the curriculum never prepared them for. By then, the course is long over.

The research on institutional alignment points in the same direction. When learning outcomes are assessed at the program level with shared vocabulary and shared rubrics, graduates demonstrate stronger capacity for judgment in novel situations. When outcomes are left to individual interpretation, the variance in preparation grows wider with each course that does not assess them.

Source: Clover Park Technical College. CWLO Toolkit: Critical Thinking. Teaching and Learning Center, 2024.

WHY THIS MATTERS

In 2005, Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe published the second edition of Understanding by Design, a framework for curriculum planning built around a single diagnostic question: what do we want students to be able to do with what they have learned, in a situation they have not seen before?

The framework argues that most curriculum design works backward from content. Instructors select what to teach, deliver it, and assess whether students retained it. Wiggins and McTighe propose working forward from transfer: deciding first what students should be able to do when they encounter a novel situation, and then designing both the instruction and the assessment to build that capacity.

The transfer problem is the central finding. Skills practiced only in structured, familiar contexts do not transfer to unstructured, unfamiliar ones. A student can demonstrate mastery on every assessment in a course and still be unable to apply what they learned when the situation changes. This is not because the student is incapable. It is because the assessment never asked them to practice the thing that transfer requires: making a judgment call when the correct answer is not provided.

The practical implication for assessment design is direct. An assessment that asks a student to recall a procedure builds the habit of recall. An assessment that asks a student to evaluate two competing procedures using defined criteria and justify the selection based on real-world constraints builds a different habit entirely. Both can look rigorous. Only one builds transfer.

The most useful diagnostic question Wiggins and McTighe offer is this: if a student completed this assessment with a perfect score, what can they now do that they could not do before? If the honest answer is "recall the right information," that tells you something about the assessment, not the student.

Source: Wiggins, G. and McTighe, J. (2005). Understanding by Design, 2nd Ed. ASCD.

WHY THIS MATTERS

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report is one of the most widely cited analyses of workforce skill demand. The 2025 edition, drawing on employer surveys across 55 economies and 22 industry sectors, identifies the skills most likely to grow in importance over the next five years as automation reshapes the labor market.

Critical thinking and complex problem-solving rank at the top of that list. Not as supplements to technical knowledge, but as the primary differentiators between roles that remain human and roles that do not. The report's core finding is consistent across editions: the tasks most vulnerable to automation are those with predictable inputs and defined protocols. The tasks that remain human are those requiring judgment under uncertainty.

This has direct implications for technical education. Programs that train students in field-specific procedures are preparing students for the ninety percent. That work is essential. But the credential signals readiness for the ten percent as well: the situations no protocol anticipated, the decisions that require a person to think rather than execute. If the program did not assess that capacity, the signal the credential sends is incomplete.

The report also notes that employer expectations around critical thinking are not limited to management-level roles. Across every sector surveyed, employers identified the ability to reason about competing options, weigh tradeoffs, and communicate the rationale for a decision as skills they cannot train on the job. They expect those capacities to arrive with the credential.

For community college programs, the implication is clear. Students entering technical, healthcare, logistics, and public-service careers are entering workplaces where routine work is increasingly automated and the remaining work increasingly requires judgment. What they practiced in the classroom is what they will bring to the first call they have to make without a manual.

Source: World Economic Forum. (2025). Future of Jobs Report 2025. World Economic Forum.

I ran the trace back ten years. Not to a single instructor. Not to a single bad course. To the accumulation of decisions about what to assess and what to leave out.

Nomis was not negligent. He was doing exactly what the system asked of him. The system never asked him to name the skills he was already teaching. It never built the naming into the rubric. It never gave Alice a chance to practice the skill as a skill rather than a behavior.

When I mapped the gap, it was not dramatic. It was invisible. That is what the Eraser does. It does not erase dramatically. It erases gradually. One assessment at a time. One course at a time. Until the credential means something different from what the employer thinks it means.

Alice's role was designed to hold. The assessment was not designed to build what the role required.

WHY THIS MATTERS

MIT's Work of the Future Task Force has published research on the relationship between automation and labor market outcomes since 2019. Their 2023 report offers one of the most precise analyses available of which human capacities are at genuine risk and which are not.

The report challenges the framing of AI as a straightforward job eliminator. The more accurate picture, based on longitudinal labor market data, is that AI functions as a sorting mechanism. It accelerates the obsolescence of roles built primarily around procedural execution and information retrieval. It creates demand for roles built around judgment, coordination, and the evaluation of AI-generated outputs.

The capacities that survive this sorting are not rare. They are the skills the CWLO names: the ability to assess relevance and validity when evidence is incomplete, to weigh feasibility and efficiency when both matter, to identify which stakeholders are affected by a decision and in what ways. These are the capacities that remain genuinely human when the algorithm has done everything the algorithm can do.

The report also raises a concern specific to credentialing systems. When programs certify students as job-ready without assessing the judgment capacities the role actually requires, the credential creates a false signal. The graduate arrives with documentation implying readiness. The employer acts on that signal. The gap appears only when the situation demands more than the credential ever assessed.

The assessment is not a classroom formality in this account. It is the mechanism by which a student either develops a capacity or does not. What gets assessed gets practiced. What does not get assessed does not transfer. That is the whole story.

Source: MIT Work of the Future Task Force. (2023). Shaping the Future of Work. MIT.
