Sex in Romantic Comedy

This week I watched two John Cusack movies that I’ve always seen the posters for but have never seen. I mistakenly started with High Fidelity (2000, R) then backward in time to Say Anything (1989, PG-13). In hindsight, I wish I’d watch them in release order, because watching Cusack’s depressed, cynical record-store owner before his innocent, boombox-wielding teen made the latter feel like a bittersweet prequel; a glimpse of the youthful idealism that would eventually harden into midlife disillusionment. There are many similarities in these two films beyond their lead actor. Still, viewing them out of order highlighted the similarities and differences in how each film treats love, relationships, and especially sex.

Both film feature Cusack as a romantically inclined, emotionally intense protagonist. Yet the tone, mood and sexual expression in each reflect the cultural and cinematic climate of their time. Say Anything was released at the tail end of the 1980s, a time when teen comedies, like Fast Times at Ridgemont High or Porky’s, were filled with hormones, but often lacked emotional depth. This film stands out with its emotional honesty. Lloyd Dobler isn’t chasing a hookup; he’s chasing connection. When he and Diane have sex, the scene is quiet, respectful, and emotionally charged. The camera focuses on their faces, not their bodies. It’s an act of intimacy, not spectacle. In line with the PG-13 rating, Say Anything handles sex with care, using it as a turning point in the characters emotional journey rather than a comedic punchline or visual lure.

In contrast, High Fidelity, rated R and released in 2000, opens up a very different space for sexual storytelling. Rob Gordon, Cusack’s character, is an aging hipster reflecting on his failed relationships and floundering in his adult life. Sex in High Fidelity is part of a messy mix of love, ego, and insecurity. It’s talked about openly and with a candid, sometimes crude sense of humor (Jack Black was best at this and did a kick-ass job in this movie). While Say Anything treated sex as a delicate rite of passage, High Fidelity treats it as reoccurring theme that focusing on sex makes it problematic for the main character to function in everyday life. The R rating allows for more explicit dialogue and situations, but the real maturity lies in how the film use sex to explore emotional responsibility and self-reflection.

What’s fascinating is how both films, despite their differences, showcase men grappling with love and identity. Both characters are emotionally vulnerable, prone to dramatic gestures (iconic speaker up in front of the car), and deeply introspective. But while Lloyd is hopeful, Rob is haunted. Say Anything reflects a late-80’s optimism, where love can conquer doubt. High Fidelity, shaped by Gen X cynicism and the rise of indie realism, suggests that love is work, and its mostly self-work. Visually, the evolution is also clear. Say Anything is lit warmly, with wide, romantic shots of LAKEWOOD. High Fidelity is darker, grittier with tighter frames that reflect Rob’s internal chaos. Together these films show not only that John Cusack’s characters evolved, but how romantic comedies grew up alongside their audience.
