Character in Independent Film

Watching Reservoir Dogs (1992) and Juno (2007) back-to-back really got me thinking about how independent films can feel so different, yet still be grounded in strong writing and interesting characters. The two couldn’t be more different on the surface, but I found myself drawn to the voices behind each them. Each film delivers fast, witty dialogue and memorable characters with strong internal conflicts. However, the eras in which they were made significantly influence the tone, character focus, and what the films ultimately choose to say.

Reservoir Dogs came out in the early 1990s, a time when indie films were exploding. Cinephile Quentin Tarantino’s debut stood out to me as raw and rebellious, like he was breaking all the rules on purpose. His colorful cast of characters are not friendly, but they are engaging. Mr. Pink arguing about tipping instantly hooked me and helped to paint a picture of how the rest of the cast will act and react during the rest of the movie. I’m always impressed at Tarantino’s ability to hook the audience with cool characters saying cool lines and doing cool things. All he needs is to rent out a diner and he’s got a memorable scene. Something I noticed on this viewing after our lesson is that the characters don’t really grow in this movie, rather they unravel into their true self once their put in a stressful and paranoid situation. Mr. White starts out as trustworthy mentor figure that just wants to help Mr. Orange survive, but by then end, he’s even yelling and pointing a gun at him. There’s no need for special effects or expensive sets, just pure character tension and razor-sharp writing.

Another movie that was filmed with similar budget constraints is Juno, but the two films differ wildly in tone and purpose. Where Reservoir Dogs is a gritty, ironic, and violent, Juno is sweet, awkward, and emotionally open. Released in the late 2000s, Juno reflects a different cultural moment, where people are more interested in vulnerability, identity, and finding humor in personal pain. Even though the stakes aren’t life or death, I still felt invested because of how relatable Juno is. Juno MacGuff is a smart, fast-talking teenager who uses sarcasm to mask her fear and insecurity. Over the course of the film, though, we see her mature emotionally. She starts off making quick, dismissive decisions, but by the end, she’s thinking about others in a more grounded way. Her growth feels earned. In one of the most quoted lines, Juno says, “I don’t really know what kind of girl I am.” In my opinion, this moment speaks volumes about the character confronting their own internal confusion, which drives the entire story.

When I think about both films, I realize they’re shaped a lot by the time they came out. Reservoir Dogs is built on mistrust, ego, and nihilism, reflecting the edgy coolness of the 90s. Juno, by contrast, is about connection, vulnerability, and learning to grow up, which felt more like the focus of society at the time. What they share is a belief that character comes first, and that when characters are compelling enough, you don’t need explosions or CGI to keep people watching. You just need a good script and a story that lets the characters show us who they really are. That’s one thing I’ve come to really appreciate about indie films.
