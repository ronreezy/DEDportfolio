Genre Studies - 2001: A Space Odyssey

Watching Stanley Kubrick’s 2001: A Space Odyssey is like stepping into a dream I’m not sure I understand but can’t stop thinking about. From the opening shots of tapirs in the desert to the slow drift through space, this film challenges everything I’ve come to expect from sci-fi. As I sat with each scene, I thought about what the film was trying to say about the history of humanity, technology and even art itself. There’s something eerie and awe-inspiring about the films deliberate use of silence, music and refusal to rely on dialogue. It made me reflect not just on the story, but on the time it came out, the risks it took and how much it asks from the viewer. This paper is my attempt to unpack all of that, through the different lenses we explored in class. I don’t have all of the answers, but I do have a lot of thoughts about how this film made me feel and wonder.

2001: A Space Odyssey doesn’t seem too political on the surface, but I couldn’t help noticing the film quietly reflects the Cold War era it was made in. There’s a scene early on, around the lunar base meeting, where Dr. Floyd is being super cagey about the monolith discovery. That moment gave me a sense of how real-world governments sometimes withhold information “for the good of the public”. Especially during the tense political times of the sixties. Even HAL’s cold, calculating decisions could be seen as reflecting fears of systems or governments that follow logic over humanity. I feel like Kubrick is asking the question to the audience about where we’re going as a species and if our institutions are helping or hurting that progress. Coincidentally, generative AI is making people grapple with the same dilemma these days.

Gender isn’t a major focus in 2001, but its absence kind of becomes the point. Almost every person with authority or dialogue in this movie is a man. The women, when they show up, are receptionists or stewardesses. Even the space station receptionist is more of a set piece than a character. That stood out to me, especially watching it from today’s perspective. I kept thinking, “Was this just normal for the time, or was it Kubrick deliberately showing a future where traditional gender roles hadn’t changed much?” There’s a short scene where Dr. Floyd video calls his daughter, and while it’s sweet, it also reinforces the idea that family and caregiving are separate from the serious work of space exploration. It’s not offensive; it just seems a bit dated for a movie that’s supposed to be about the future. The film left me thinking about how our visions of the future are shaped by the people who imagine them.

There’s almost no sexuality in this movie, which felt strange at first. No romantic tension, no flirting, not even any chemistry between the characters. That absence actually makes me think a lot about how the future humans might prioritize logic and professionalism over emotional or sexual connection. Maybe Kubrick was imagining a future where desire has been “evolved out” of use or at least compartmentalized. On the flip side, maybe he didn’t think it had a place in a movie about big ideas like evolution and artificial intelligence. Either way, it left the film feeling emotionally cold to me.

Even though the effects aren’t flashy by today’s standards, I was blown away by how timeless some of the set pieces are. The rotating hallway scene where Bowman jog upside-down still holds up. I actually had to pause the movie and look up how they shot it. It’s all practical effects, which is wild. There’s no space battle, no big monster alien and no planet being blown up. Instead, the budget goes toward realism and mood. The sound design and model were especially impressive given the technological limitations of special effects of the 1960s. The ships move slowly and silently, which I guess is how they would really move in space. It gave the movie this eerie, floating quality that reminded me of Interstellar, which came out almost 50 years later. The sets feel clean and futuristic without being overdesigned. To be honest, a lot of shots in this movie look better than a lot of the CGI slop we’ve been fed at the beginning of the 21st century.

It's kind of ironic that the most compelling character in the movie isn’t a person, it’s HAL. He’s creepy, but also weirdly sympathetic. When HAL says, “I’m afraid, Dave,” I felt more emotion that I did from any of the human characters. Bowman and Poole are professional and competent, but not particularly expressive. I kept wondering Kubrick did that on purpose, to show how humans may be robotic, while the machines become more “alive”. HAL is manipulative, sure, but he also seems curious and maybe even insecure. That made me feel something. The humans, by contrast, are blank slates. They don’t argue, joke or panic. They just go through the motions. It was hard to connect with them, which made the film seem more like a philosophical essay than a movie.

Kubrick avoids obvious stereotypes, which I appreciated. There’s no comic relief character, no jock, no nerd, no villain in the traditional sense. The characters are stripped of personality, almost to the point of abstraction. But that also means there is not much diversity. Everyone is white. Everyone speaks in the same calm, careful tone. The future portrayed in 2001 is clean, professional, and sterile. I don’t think it’s intentional, and more of just a product of it’s time, but it seems like this future is designed for a specific type a person: white, male, educated, emotionless. In trying to create a “universal” story, Kubrick ends up erasing a lot of human variety.

This movie is definitely art. There are whole stretches of the movie with no dialogue, no characters, just music and visuals. I had to check a minute in to see if there was something wrong with the video file. Some of the sequences in the movie feel more experimental than a traditional movie. I’m still tripping watching him transport through the stargate. I had to slow down watching this movie. I realized early on that the movie is more vibes than action. The docking sequence set to classical music made me feel like I was watching a space ballet. It was beautiful, but also a little hypnotic. That said, there were moments where I felt restless. I’m used to movies moving faster, with more emotion and conflict. This felt like an exhibition in a museum. It’s clear that Kubrick was more interested in mood, pacing and ideas over traditional story beats and action.

There are so many things to think related to themes in this movie. Evolution seems like the biggest theme, with how we started with the apes and the introduction of the first tool, to the iconic flip to nuclear satellites in space. The monolith is a kind of mystery box that triggers transformation, but we’re never told why or how. It reminded me of the briefcase from Pulp Fiction. I like these kinds of mysteries. They keep me wondering and guessing. To me, the monolith seems like some sort of intelligence transferor that takes human knowledge to the next level. This expansion of human intellect can be mirrored with the advancement of artificial intellect as well. HAL’s breakdown made me think about consciousness, trust and whether the machines of the future can really feel. What happens when the tools we use get so advanced that they start to pose a threat to mankind? The Terminator and the Matrix come to mind…

The structure of 2001 threw me at first. It’s more like four loosely connected short films, than one cohesive narrative. There’s the ape sequence, the moon base, the HAL section and the stargate transformation. Each part has its own tone and pacing. It’s not the traditional hero’s journey. There is no protagonist or goal, just a feeling of moving forward through time and space. I didn’t mind the lack of exposition. In fact, I liked having to figure things out on my own. It gave me an opportunity to spark up a conversation with my colleague, Geoff Cain, to talk about what he felt the stargate sequence was supposed to mean. The film trusts the audience to be patient and observant. It’s not about what happens next, it’s about what it all means. Even if I don’t understand everything, I know I’ve experienced something that will stay with me, which I think is the purpose of any great piece of art.
