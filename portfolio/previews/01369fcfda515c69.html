Budgets in Science Fiction

This week I watched two science fiction films from opposite sides of the budget spectrum: Waterworld (1995), a notoriously expensive post-apocalyptic adventure, and Attack the Block (2011), a low-budget urban alien invasion film. Both use the sci-fi genre to speculate about survival and human behavior under pressure, but their approaches to story, visuals, and scale reveal how budget can shape a film’s identity.

With a reported production budget of around $175 million, Waterworld was the most expensive movie ever made at the time. The film used massive real-world sets, elaborate stunt work, and practical effects on open water to create a flooded Earth. Despite its financial infamy, the film’s use of physical scale and detailed world-building is impressive. I watched this movie a lot when I was young (it was just some random VHS my parents bought) and I was always in awe of the expansive views of the ocean, colossal floating cities, and intricate machinery workings of Costner’s boat. He’s always fixing something, which makes the dystopian world feel tangible. The costume design is ragged and looks lived-in, reflecting the scavenger lifestyle the last people on Earth had to endure. And obviously, the choreography work with the jet skis is king. If that was what the budget was spent on, it was well worth it.

Thematically, Waterworld taps into environmental anxieties and survivalist fantasies. Its large budget allows the filmmakers to create a fully fleshed-out world with extensive lore and spectacle, but sometimes this emphasis overshadows character development. The story is broad in scope but somewhat traditional in that it follows the lone antihero, a child with a map salvation, and an eye-patched villain in leather (Dennis Hopper’s eye haunted me while I was young).

Attack the Block, by contrast, reportedly had a budget of under $15 million. Set mostly within a South London housing project, the film turns its financial limitations into strengths. The story unfolds over the course of one night and follows a group of teens who have to defend their block from alien invaders. The monsters are barely visible, besides their glowing teeth, making them terrifying while cleverly sidestepping expensive CGI. Lighting is used strategically to obscure details and create suspense. Instead of elaborate sets, like in Waterworld, the film uses stairwells, elevators, and courtyards to ground the action in a familiar yet heightened reality.

Despite its small budget, Attack the Block delivers some rich social commentary on youth culture, race, class and media perception. The kids begin as petty criminals (led by ya’ boy FN-2187, John Boyega), but by the end, they’re heroes in their own community. That emotional arc feels earned, even without the need for big special effects. The camera often sticks close to the characters, keeping the tension high and the action personal. This movie is less about scale and more about stakes.

Worldworld and Attack the Block are two examples that expensive does not be good and that ideas are better than spectacle. Spectacle definitely has its place, but when it’s at the expense of good storytelling, you lose the emotional stakes and don’t care if the world gets sucked into a huge hole in the sky. Either way, I still like these movies because I love the sci-fi genre, no matter the budget. Though many people don’t like Waterworld, I reacted positively to the message of human resilience and felt the same while watching Attack the Block. Budget shapes the tools filmmakers use, but it doesn’t define the heart of the story.
