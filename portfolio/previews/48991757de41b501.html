Story and Theme in Noir

I watched two movies this week: Akira Kurosawa’s 1949 Noir classic, Stray Dog and Shane Black’s new-Noir romp, Kiss Kiss Bang Bang. Though set in different times with vastly different plots, I could find similarities in the themes and messaging each are trying to convey; frenetic male leads being thrown into situations that are way over their heads that get help from cool, calm mentors. And pistols.

Stray Dog is set in Japan after the war, and the movie really leans into how hot summers in Japan are. I visited Tokyo in 2015 and can tell you that all the scenes with people sweating and using hand fans to survive is real. Granted, there is much more air conditioning now, but the heat outside is still oppressive. Kurosawa, like in a lot of his movies, uses nature elements like the weather as an integral part of the mise en scene. I think it is used in this movie to amplify the stress and intensity the main character, Murakami, feels throughout the movie as he tries to track down his gun. Sato, the more experienced detective, tells him that these kinds of things happen and acts as the calm mentor figure, but Murakami still feels guilty and responsible for all the bad things that happened because he let his gun get stolen.

Kiss Kiss Bang Bang on the other hand has a completely different tone but still hints at the theme of someone who is caught in a tough situation and is trying to navigate their way through it. It’s much louder and fast paced, but at the core, it’s a story about Harry, played by a pre-Iron Man, post drug addict, Robert Downey Jr., who doesn’t belong in the world he’s been thrown into. When he gets to LA after a chance casting call in New York, he finds guidance from a cool, calm private investigator played by Val Kilmer (RIP). The story then gets crazy with betrayal, violence, and some dark moments (both figuratively and literally), hallmarks of the noir genre, but it’s wrapped up in jokes and wacky dialogue. I laughed out lot a lot in this movie. Perry’s secret pistol scene near the end is so good.

And speaking about pistols, while Stray Dog has it as main focal point in the plot, Kiss Kiss Bang Bang features pistols throughout and both have a scene where the main protagonist gets shot at the end. Murakami gets grazed during the final conformation, and Harry takes a bullet after what feels like the 20th plot twist. Guns aren’t just props in these stories; they are symbols of guilt, danger and power, and both movies use them to show how close these characters are to losing control, or losing themselves.

These two films might be completely different in tone, language, and style, but they both show how noir doesn’t need to be stuck in one time period one setting. You can have a sweaty Japanese detective drama and a sarcastic Hollywood buddy-crime movie, and both still capture the feeling of being lost, overwhelmed, and trying to hold onto some kind of moral center, with the help of a mentor and a well-placed pistol.
